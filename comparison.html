Comparing Pose Estimation for Exercise Analysis

Listen to Page Overview

Pose-estimation systems should be evaluated based on the needs of the application. For exercise analysis, the most relevant considerations are landmark representation, real-time processing, deployment environment, and the type of movement being analyzed.

Landmarks and Exercise Analysis

Landmark count alone does not determine whether a model is better for exercise analysis. What matters is whether the model provides the anatomical landmarks required by the application.

For a squat, the hip, knee, and ankle landmarks are enough to calculate a knee angle, which can be used to understand movement phase and repetition patterns. Other exercises or more detailed biomechanical analysis may need additional landmarks or spatial information.

Real-Time Processing

An exercise-analysis application typically processes a continuous stream of frames while keeping landmark estimates stable enough for downstream calculations. This makes temporal continuity and computational cost important practical considerations.

In general, there is a tradeoff between computational cost and the amount of pose information produced. The choice of system depends on whether the application prioritizes lightweight tracking, richer structure, or multi-person assignment.

Single-Person vs. Multi-Person Scenarios

A home workout application may mainly need to follow one person in a fixed field of view. In that case, the main requirement is often accurate tracking of a single individual over time.

A gym, sports, or group-training environment may require distinguishing multiple people and assigning the correct keypoints to each person. This creates different design priorities and helps explain why some systems emphasize lightweight single-person tracking while others are built around multi-person association.

Which Approach Fits This Tutorial?

For the squat-analysis example developed later in this tutorial, MediaPipe / BlazePose is a natural fit because it emphasizes real-time body landmarks and on-device applications.[2]

However, this does not make it universally superior. YOLO Pose can be useful when pose estimation is integrated with a broader YOLO-based vision pipeline,[3] while OpenPose illustrates a strong multi-person bottom-up strategy for scenes that require person association.[4]

Key takeaways: the best pose-estimation system depends on what information the application needs, where the model will run, and whether it must analyze one person or several. For exercise analysis, pose estimation is only the first step: the landmark coordinates still need to be converted into meaningful measurements such as joint angles and movement states.
